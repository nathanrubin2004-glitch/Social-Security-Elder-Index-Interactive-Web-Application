Background

Social Security is the foundation of retirement income for most older Americans — yet whether it covers the real cost of living independently is rarely measured against a realistic budget.

The Elder Index™ (UMass Boston Gerontology Institute) estimates what adults aged 65+ truly need to meet basic needs — housing, food, health care, and transportation. Unlike the federal poverty line, it varies by state, household type, housing status, and health.

Research question: What share of the Elder Index does the average Social Security benefit cover — nationally, across all 50 states + DC — and how has that gap changed from 2015 to 2025?
2

Data & Methods

  • Elder Index 2025 — annual cost of living for older adults, by state × household × housing × health (elderindex.org).
  • Social Security benefits — average annual benefit for retired workers, December 2023 (SSA OASDI Beneficiaries by State & County, Pub. 13-11954).
  • Coverage = average Social Security benefit ÷ Elder Index, for every jurisdiction and household/housing/health combination.
  • Annual gap = Elder Index − benefit (the unmet need).
  • State-level couple benefits are estimated from each state's single benefit × the national couple-to-single ratio (no published state figure exists).
3

Why the Gap Barely Moved

Social Security's annual cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) tracks general inflation (CPI-W). But the Elder Index tracks the costs older adults actually face — housing and health care — which rose just as fast. Benefits and needs climbed in step, so coverage held near 70% while the dollar shortfall widened — up more than 45% in a decade.

Social Security remains the foundation of retirement income — but for millions of older adults it does not fully cover the basics of housing, health care, food, and transportation.
Implications: Coverage gaps persist and the dollar shortfall is widening, with the heaviest burden on single older renters in high-cost states. Measuring adequacy against the Elder Index — not the poverty line — reveals needs that aggregate statistics hide, informing benefit, housing, and health-care policy.
